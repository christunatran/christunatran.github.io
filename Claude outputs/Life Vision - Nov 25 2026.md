# Life vision (draft) — Nov 25, 2026 (updated Nov 26)

Built from your Nov 25–26 messages, the Nov 9 Career Direction doc, the Feb 23 "vision after goals" doc, your About me doc, and your Second Brain notes. Structure, the tensions, and anything marked as my read are my synthesis. Edit anything that doesn't sound like you.

## North star
Help people take their attention back, and make it fun. Build interfaces, objects, installations, and content that make the distracting digital paradigm feel optional, and that leave people more thoughtful and grounded than they were before. Humor and whimsy are the way in. Influence hearts and minds the way Sam Lavigne and Tega Brain do, but for a wide audience, not mainly the art world. The medium doesn't have to be art.

The through-line across everything below: **understand the technology and heritage we take for granted, and hand that understanding back to people in a form they'll actually pick up.**

## Who it's for
You want work that resonates with a lot of people, not mainly overeducated urbanites who treat indie film and contemporary art as the biggest deal when few people engage with either. Your own notes already answer this better than a mission statement would.

- Your Aug 20 note on the biggest problems of our time names the people you're worried about: the elderly and people with low digital literacy, children and the impressionable, and people with limited English proficiency. None of those are art-world people. **That list is your audience. Keep it in front of you.**
- Red Burns' original ITP is your model, not ITP as it is now. Her students taught Washington Heights residents to use video to pressure city hall for a traffic light; she built two-way cable so seniors in Reading, PA could reach each other and social services. You wrote that ITP "devolved into a self-centered art program," counter to that vision. Hold that standard against your own work.
- Your thesis intention says it directly: don't make it overly about yourself, and don't be woe-is-me. Draw from the world, not deeper into your own mind.
- Practical tests: could your parents or your brother explain what it does after one try? Does it work in Vietnamese, or for a non-native English speaker? Can someone who isn't you afford it?
- Price, venue, and language are design decisions, not afterthoughts.

## What you care about

### 1. Attention, interfaces, and friction
Screen addiction, interfaces, and "convenience" or seamlessness are on your biggest-problems list. You're after what *Good Screens* calls a healthy relationship with tech: assisting instead of doing it for you, oiling the track without changing the course. Louise Macfadyen's line is the operating principle: there's a difference between friction that wastes time and friction that prevents bad decisions. You already have shipped ideas here — a new-tab page that leads to a distraction journal, detecting "bored and clicking around" and intervening, the daily briefing app.

You're also skeptical of your own field: you wrote that over-indexing on UI and motion design is a brain drain, because in a business context it exists to maximize screen engagement, and talented people could be getting others *off* screens and back to each other.

### 2. Media literacy, and the false objectivity of AI
Media literacy is on the problems list, and so is social media and AI as a threat to democracy, plus internet and AI scams. From your reading notes: the textbox UI projects authority and objectivity that AI outputs don't have, a "93% confidence" is false precision that manufactures trust, and the ELIZA effect does the rest. *Data Feminism* makes the same argument about data. This is a design problem, not just a discourse problem, which is what makes it yours.

### 3. History of technology and cultural heritage
You believe understanding where things came from makes people more thoughtful and grounded. "Museums are like history books we can walk through." "Chairs have reflected the technology of their time" — and the reason to build a chair or a lamp is that everyone already knows what one *should* be, so you can ask what it *could* be. Hito Steyerl's method is the one you've been learning: use critical theory to reveal the power structures inside seemingly neutral technologies, and ask who benefits. Barbara Kruger does it in four words.

### 4. Vietnam, diaspora, and Việt Kiều identity
Your thesis is here: the revolving Đèn kéo quân with local Vietnamese-to-English translation, northern Vietnamese craft, propaganda, Việt Kiều identity. So is the plastic stool — born of necessity and affordability, and now carrying meaning for immigrant families. So is your family lore: your dad sent away at 15 as a carpentry apprentice, ranked first in school but couldn't afford past middle school, your grandfather five years a prisoner of war. You want to be a bridge between Vietnam and the US and take part in Vietnam's growth. You also feel white-washed and want to undo some of it.

**This is the strongest answer to your classism worry.** It's not a taste culture. It's your family and millions of people like them.

### 5. Community, and lowering the friction to gathering
Your problems list ends with "lowering the friction to community gathering; creating infrastructure to make community gathering easier." You've critiqued American individualism and neoliberalism for producing people who never experience community, and you've started small versions yourself — dinners, a housewarming, TIRT with friends, Analog Mondays in the Feb vision. Your collective references are the Propeller Group, Fluxus, Balming Tiger, and MSCHF.

### 6. Ownership, repair, and modding over consumption
Framework, Punkt, Modem's Terra and Heirloom Hardware. The cyberdeck as, in that report's words, a subversive grab at ownership and control in the face of corporate rapaciousness — tempered by your own caution that hardware choices mean little without addressing the underlying systems of power. Against: consumerism, materialism, premium mediocre, bullshit jobs, being "spiritually chopped."

### 7. Anti-personalization and echo chambers
From the Pinterest note: you and Gabe searched the same word and got different worlds, and what you build is shaped by what you're shown. You concluded that hyper-personalization shouldn't be normalized and asked whether it can be turned off. That's an unbuilt project sitting right in your wheelhouse.

### 8. Play, humor, and the inner animal
The clown workshop changed you — you reframed "inner child" as inner animal, and noted how much an urban landscape and social constructs repress us without our noticing. Comedy and stand-up are on the list. Whimsy is your way in, not decoration.

### 9. Craft and hands
You keep coming back to making with your hands when heady work pulls you away from it. Wood, riso, fabrication, physical computing, the shop. You collect tool basics on purpose rather than specializing.

### 10. Integrity
From your thesis intentions: talk about it with everyone, make an artifact, commit and follow through, don't present smoke and mirrors out of fear of fallout, don't centre yourself. From the cyberdeck note: only make it if you'd make it when no one's looking. From the Celine Nguyen note: obsessing over who had it easier is passivity in disguise.

### 11. The rest of a life
Faith and church on Sundays. Family and taking care of them. Your body — gym, the 145 squat. Friends and making work with them. Love and fun. Money as runway, not as score. Not being famous or rich, but knowing how to ship things and do it again.

## Tensions to be honest about
1. **You will use the platforms you critique.** Content is distribution, but doomscrolling is the thing you're fighting. Decide the rules now — cadence, no feed time, where you post. You already have "no phone in bedroom" on a habit list.
2. **Entrepreneurship versus your own critique.** You've written that you have a problem with artists doing commerce under the guise of art, and that an artist whose art is one node in a business empire isn't an artist. You also want to sell products. These coexist if you keep the labels honest: a product sold as a product is clean; a piece of art used as marketing is the thing you object to. Decide which each thing is before you launch it, not after.
3. **Five threads, one burnt-out person.** Run at most two active threads per semester and rotate. Your Nov 9 note already says the load is too much.
4. **A PhD only serves threads 1 and 5.** It does not help products, comedy, or content, and it takes years. Worth it only if an attention-and-literacy question is one you can't drop. At UW's TAT Lab, the PhD students' public bios center on health, justice, and critical studies, and I found few on attention or social-media design.
5. **Comedy needs reps.** Without stage time it stays a wish. It needs a schedule.
6. **Influence needs distribution.** Lavigne and Brain are known mostly through art and press, which is a small audience. Intention won't widen it. Your defaults — NYC, ITP, galleries, art-school friends — will pull you back there unless you pick venues, platforms, and price points on purpose.
7. **Digital minimalism can be a class marker.** Device-free meetups and analog objects can read as a lifestyle for people with time and money. Something that works for a person with a long commute, two jobs, or a phone they need for work is harder and more useful.
8. **Critique isn't the same as building for someone.** A lot of your sharpest writing is against a class you belong to. It's good fuel and it clarifies your values, but a critique has a target while a product has a user. The work that reaches people will be built *for* your dad, your brother, a kid, a grandmother — not *against* your roommate. My read, not yours: this is the single biggest lever on the reach you say you want.
