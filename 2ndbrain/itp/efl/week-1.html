How VC’s Make Decisions

tl;dr jockey (team) > horse (business/strategy)

deal sourcing & selection

  • generating deal flow: VCs connecting with start-ups that are looking for funding
  • VCs rely on their networks to source opportunities
    • entrepreneurs should research who belongs to a VC’s network and try to get an introduction from someone in it
    • because management team weighs so heavily in investment decisions, entrepreneurs who are not plugged into venture networks may face hurdles
  • for VCs, having a clear message about what you will and will not do, how you provide real venture assistance, and how you approach bold visions is key to winning these types of opportunities
  • how VCs deliver alpha (in order of importance):
    1. deal sourcing — most critical
    2. deal selection
    3. post-investment actions

jockey vs. horse

  • jockey = entrepreneurial team
  • horse = start-up’s strategy and business model
  • strength of founding team is more important than the start-up’s strategy or business model
  • what contributes most to success/failure of portfolio companies: management team — most critical

term sheet structure

typical VC term sheets ensure the entrepreneur will do very well financially if they perform, but investors can take control of the business if the entrepreneur doesn’t deliver:

  • cash flow rights — the financial upside that gives founders incentives to perform
  • control rights — the board and voting rights that allow VCs to intervene if needed
  • liquidation rights — the distribution of the payoff if the company flounders and has to be sold
  • employment terms, particularly vesting — gives entrepreneurs incentives both to perform and stay at the company

investment terms critical to VCs

  • pro rata investment rights — allow VCs to acquire an additional stake in a company and preserve their initial ownership percentage in subsequent rounds
    • “the biggest source of returns is our ability to double down on our winners”
  • liquidation preferences
  • antidilution rights
  • vesting of the founders’ equity
  • company’s valuation
  • board control — seen as the most important control mechanism

glossary

  • IRR (internal rate of return): only calculated after the fact, when there is an exit for our limited partners
  • antidilution rights: allow the number of shares that current investors hold to be adjusted if future financing rounds are done at a lower price than the current round
  • liquidation rights: ensure that if a company is sold, the VCs get paid before the founders do; typically guarantee the amount invested or some multiple of it (e.g., double)
  • option pool: an amount of equity set aside for grants to compensate current and future employees of the start-up; usually a percentage of the company’s total value
  • participation rights: allow VCs to get back the face value of their investment (or a multiple of it), then share in the value created above that level at the time of a company exit
  • pro rata investment rights: give VCs the right to invest in a company’s subsequent investment rounds and preserve their initial percentage of ownership
  • redemption rights: give VCs the ability to return stock to the company — i.e., force it to pay back the amount of the investment

universities as a source of deal flow

  • universities are often the source of innovations that end up in the portfolios of VCs
  • university officials can learn how to better leverage the innovative activity happening within their halls
  • building relationships with leading VCs and promoting an entrepreneurial community can help spur start-up activity

Should Investors Bet on the Jockey or the Horse?

tl;dr jockey (team) < horse (business/strategy)

  • examines how VC-backed companies evolve from early business plan -> IPO -> public company
  • core implication for jockey-vs-horse debate: investors in start-ups should place more weight on the business (“the horse”) than on the management team (“the jockey”), since businesses tend to stick while management gets swapped along the way
    • paper’s actual data: management turnover is common, but business lines almost never change; horse stays put and jockey is routinely swapped out

      a good business can survive bad management getting replaced, but a bad business rarely gets saved by a great team

Are “Better” Ideas More Likely to Succeed? An Empirical Analysis of Startup Evaluation

tl;dr horse > jockey holds especially well in R&D-intensive sectors, precisely because those businesses coalesce early around IP/technology. in sectors without that hard IP anchor, the “horse” is much less legible early on.

core finding: it depends on the sector

  • in R&D-intensive sectors (life sciences, hardware, energy): ideas that got more mentor interest were significantly more likely to reach commercialization and raise real funding
    • why? tend to form around concrete, hard-to-change intellectual assets (patents, academic research, core technology) — the idea itself is the critical resource, so it’s assessable on paper
    • careful upfront screening of the idea itself is worthwhile — the fixed costs of getting it wrong are high, and the idea is diagnostic
  • in non-R&D-intensive sectors (consumer web/mobile, enterprise software, consumer products): mentor interest had basically no predictive power at all — reading the idea alone told you nothing about whether it would succeed
    • why? form more around the founders’ human capital and pivot/adapt heavily as they go — so an early idea summary doesn’t capture what the company will actually become
    • upfront idea-screening doesn’t tell you much — a “spray and pray” approach (small bets, let it play out) or vetting the founding team may be more efficient, since the business will likely look very different by the time it succeeds
