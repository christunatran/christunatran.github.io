just vibe-coding won't get you a job

Sep 04, 2026

quotes thoughts ai drafts

“Understanding software fundamentals allows you to recognize what tradeoffs even exist.”

— Andrew Ng

I agree. In this age of vibe-coding, I don’t think it’s as necessary to be adept in lower-level systems programming or syntax, but it’s still important, and perhaps even more important, to study software architecture, design patterns, existing off-the-shelf/open-source solutions to understand the tradeoffs, not constantly be reinventing the wheel, write better prompts, and effectively make better decisions. especially with how every AI prompt costs tokens with financial, environmental, and societal costs that have been heavily abstracted away from our daily view.

What sets apart an experienced software engineer from a novice is the ability to understand the bigger picture of the software they’re building— knowing how to go beyond the MVP/POC and optimize, whether that’s for performance, latency, saving compute costs, etc.

When I was working at IBM doing data engineering and machine learning, the key to getting promoted was not simply writing better code. It was being able to draw software and cloud architecture diagrams for the products we were selling and explain them to stakeholders, and to be able to justify decisions made in the software design.


Stuff like this was my bread and butter that kept me employable at the time.

I think the best ways to learn these skills are to:

  1. Work in a software job that requires you to collaborate on a large codebase with a team
  2. Work on open-source projects that require you to make pull requests
  3. Work on a longterm personal project where you are regularly committing to GitHub, sharing with real-world users, collecting feedback, and delivering improvements and bug fixes
  4. General curiosity about how the things you’re building work, rather than just accepting whatever Claude Code throws at you.
  5. Read the following: “Designing Data-Intensive Applications” by Martin Kleppmann, “Fundamentals of Data Engineering” by Joe Reis, “Designing AI interfaces” by Louise Macfadyen, “Designing Machine Learning Systems” by Chip Huyen, Design Patterns (FYI, these books are all free if you log into O’Reilly using your NYU email!)
    • Note: if you’re spending all of your time reading and not really writing code, then you’re just LARPing and not actually learning
  6. Stop listening to the BS that people are spewing on X/Twitter. Most are out-of-touch tech bros who think the world revolves around SF, got no bitchez, and are no fun at parties.

Overall, the computer science degree is still really useful. Anyone who says that it’s pointless to study CS because of AI is fear-mongering and doesn’t know what they’re talking about. It’s only pointless to study CS for the people who go through the motions and get their degree without actually learning anything. But if you do it right, it’s an invaluable skillset to, at some level, understand how computers and OS’s work, how networks work, data structures and algorithms, optimization and the math involved, networks and cloud architecture, etc. Of course this background is going to give you a leg up, especially with vibe-coding. (See below for what Andrew Ng had to say about how it helps you give coding agents better context.)

But at the same time, if you didn’t study CS, you’re not necessarily cooked either. There’s a lot of types of work out there that you just need to open your mind to.

Just vibe-coding MVPs, web art, POCs for yourself isn’t going to get you there.

To my peers at ITP/IMA,

making art with code with p5.js and some HTML/CSS
VS.
software engineering as a profession that will make you money and pay your bills
are two very different things.

Not saying that one is better than the other. But it’s important to understand the differences to align your expectations to the path you want to follow, whether it’s as a creative technologist, design engineer, HCI researcher, etc. To be realistic in your job search, just accept that you can’t do it all, a spray-and-pray approach isn’t effective. It’s better to be focused and selective from the start. (More on this with careering.) Wouldn’t you rather be good at one or a few things rather than be mediocre at many things?

The main thing is to view AI tools more as a collaborator rather than a solution, and to get your hands dirty and constantly be breaking and fixing code.

(concrete AI-assisted coding examples to come)

There’s a whole world beyond just frontend and “design engineering”. Back then the job of a frontend engineer could be as simple as creating buttons and implementing UIs. But every day, the job market becomes more competitive and the expectations for what you should know grow. We can’t silo ourselves as just designers or engineers now. So, it’s important to understand the big picture of the software you’re building.

Don’t let your fear and laziness get in the way of your curiosity.

more of what Andrew Ng had to say:

”… This leads to better decisions in choosing your software stack, designing system architecture, designing your data store, testing, and so on. It also leads to much better outcomes than those for an inexperienced developer who vibe codes a solution without knowing the tradeoffs their coding agent is making — which will often be poor ones, because they don’t know what context to give their coding agent. Understanding software engineering fundamentals lets you make good tradeoffs by steering coding agents using the precise language of software engineering.

AI also gives you the opportunity to take on greater ownership and agency than before. You can identify interesting problems and opportunities, and execute to take advantage of them in responsible ways. Taking advantage of this opportunity requires knowing how to drive projects forward. For example, knowing when to quickly build an MVP to take to users for testing, and when to slow down and take longer in order to build more carefully.”
