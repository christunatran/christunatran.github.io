3. Designing for AI Inputs

“This recurrent optimization behavior reflects the knowledge gained in the nearly 25 years since Google launched: how to structure a good query for Google. And it’s exactly this knowledge that users don’t yet have for AI products.”

“implicit context: the information a system gathers from the environment without being asked. Examples include the document currently open, the text a user has selected, their location, the time of day, and what they did moments ago. There is explicit prompting: the typed or spoken request that tells the system what to do. And there is direct manipulation: the sliders, selections, buttons, and gestures that let users shape AI behavior through familiar interface patterns. A single interaction might involve all three”

“The command-line interface (CLI) represented a world where the burden of communication rested entirely on the human participant, demanding that users translate their goals into the computer’s preferred vocabulary through extensive documentation, training, and practice.”

“The best input interfaces help users create effective prompts without thinking about prompt engineering, often by letting the other two channels (context and manipulation) carry some of the load.”

“context, action, results, and examples—aren’t necessary in every prompt, it’s useful to consider how they could come together in a comprehensive prompt”

“Jakob’s Law, which states that users spend most of their time on other sites and applications, so they prefer interfaces that work similarly to what they already know”

“Each addition helps some users while cluttering the interface for others”

“The challenge of choosing which actions to surface becomes more complex as AI capabilities expand.”

“The findings validate what many interface designers have observed informally: users want both the efficiency of one-click actions for routine tasks and the flexibility of natural language for complex or novel requests.”

“Information at the beginning and end of prompts receives disproportionate attention, while crucial details buried in the middle often get overlooked. Users, unaware of these architectural constraints, naturally assume that if the interface accepts their lengthy prompt, the model will process all of it equally.”

“What makes prompting particularly interesting as an interface pattern is that it inverts traditional software interaction. Instead of learning the software’s language of menus and commands, users can express their intent in their own words. The burden shifts from the user having to memorize specific procedures to the system having to interpret human intention”

“Overly long, complex prompts can actually degrade performance, as the system struggles to parse what matters most”

“Long recordings require speaker diarization (identifying who said what), handling cross-talk, managing audio quality variations, and maintaining context over extended passages. Conversational voice must find a balance between these extremes, maintaining context while responding quickly enough to feel natural. Each type demands different feedback mechanisms: commands need immediate confirmation, recordings need processing indicators and confidence scores, and conversations need subtle cues that maintain flow without interruption.”

“Adobe’s Generative Fill uses the same selection metaphors that Photoshop users have known for decades. Google Photos’ Magic Eraser employs the familiar brush-and-highlight pattern from countless photo editing apps. In each case, the interface succeeds by feeling familiar even as it enables entirely new capabilities.”

“Decades of photo-editing software, from Photoshop to Instagram, have trained users in specific interaction patterns. They expect to select objects with lasso tools, adjust boundaries with handles, and see marching ants around active selections. When AI interfaces honor these established patterns, users can focus on their creative intent rather than learning new mechanics”

“As capabilities expand to include video, 3D, and other visual modalities, the interfaces that succeed will be those that extend existing mental models rather than requiring users to develop entirely new ones”

“This scaffolding helps users succeed without requiring them to understand the technical details of model selection or parameter tuning.”

“Starter Prompts as Product Positioning”

“Users need more than a sense of what’s possible. They need to see what’s possible here. If the tool summarizes long documents, the prompt should reflect that. If it integrates with Zillow to help users browse homes, the starter prompt should showcase that integration. Generic prompts blur the boundaries between products. Specific ones make the tool’s differentiators visible from the start.”

“Throughout all of this, the goal remains consistent: to help users translate their intentions into outcomes without requiring them to understand the machinery underneath.”

4. Output: Designing the Delivery and Presentation of LLM Responses

“AI systems learn that these subword pieces carry semantic meaning, enabling them to understand new combinations they haven’t explicitly seen before.”

“Research in human-computer interaction suggests that when people understand why they’re waiting and feel confident that progress is being made, their tolerance increases—even without distraction”

“Waiting is less about the passage of time and more about how we feel while it passes.”

“theorist Paul Watzlawick postulated in his first axiom of communication that it’s impossible not to communicate: even the absence of communication says something.”

“a product, the latency itself is a form of communication. Just as silence sends a message, the delay a user experiences when waiting for a response communicates something to them: perhaps anxiety, uncertainty, or doubt about whether their request was received. A long delay can convey to users that the system is struggling, unreliable, or not responsive to their needs”

“shift toward reasoning and faster initial outputs means that, rather than providing a means for distraction (from waiting), latency now runs the risk of being a distraction. In this situation, it’s better to consider the purpose of latency to be a method for providing status—without interrupting.”

“The goal is to make progress legible enough that users can verify the system is on the right path, but not so detailed that they lose the overall story of what’s happening.”

“Tier 2 gives designers room to think about interaction and trust. The user is no longer watching from a distance; they are looking closely to understand or validate the process.”

“layers together recognize that people vary widely in how they follow progress. Some will stay close to the task, while others will delegate and check in only occasionally. Good progress communication respects both. It allows users to drift away and return, to zoom in or out, and to trust that the system will keep them oriented no matter how much attention they give it”

“Notifications reassure and inform in an abstract way, tier 1 maintains a throughline of the topmost relevant information, tier 2 with its expanded progress provides understanding, and the full record in tier 3 preserves accountability. Progress communication ultimately builds a relationship”

6. Agentic AI: Designing for Systems That Plan, Act, and Adapt

“Microsoft Bob, a friendly interface featuring the dog Rover, designed to help people with minimal computer literacy navigate their PCs without the need for a manual (see Figure 6-1). However, Bob flopped within a year, with critics complaining that the slow-running tool infantilized users.”

“Microsoft’s vision for Clippy derived from research from Stanford University showing that people tend to treat computers like social beings”

“Reflection pattern addresses a fundamental challenge with AI systems: they make confident mistakes. A model might generate code with a subtle bug, write text with factual errors, or create a plan with logical flaws. Reflection provides a mechanism for catching these errors without human oversight at every step: it’s essentially quality control built into the agent itself.”

“As noted in Chapter 4, users will wait for AI responses, but only if they understand what’s happening and why. Therefore, it’s vital to set the user’s expectations from the outset of a task that delays will be explained clearly.”

“when an agent is reflecting: should users see that process? Showing output like “Reviewing calculations… Found an error in Q3 totals… Corrected and reverified” makes the delay comprehensible and builds trust. But showing every internal critique can feel overwhelming or expose the agent’s uncertainty in ways that undermine confidence.”

“technical implementation of the Reflection pattern typically involves multiple passes. The agent generates initial output, then uses the same or a different model to critique that output against specific criteria. For code, this might mean checking syntax, testing logic, or verifying it matches requirements. For text, it might mean fact-checking claims, assessing tone, or identifying inconsistencies. Based on the critique, the agent regenerates or adjusts the output.”

“self-reflection, where a single model both generates and critiques”

“multiagent reflection, where one agent produces output and a separate critic agent reviews it.”

“Should you display the initial, unrefined output along with the polished version? That creates transparency but adds cognitive load.”

“If an agent tries to call an API and it fails, users should be made aware something happened, but they shouldn’t feel the system is broken.”

“Transparency about agentic tool use also matters for trust.”

“Designing for Visible Planning”

“human-agent coordination”

“The design challenge is to present these complexities without making plans so convoluted that users can’t evaluate them. As mentioned earlier, the levels of detail and complexity in the task will influence the visual presentation: more complicated tasks require sophisticated and editable diagram structures. In occurrences with hypotheticals or conditional logic, sometimes the right answer is to hide the possible branches unless they’re triggered: show the main path and only explain branches when the agent actually takes one.”

“The power of ReAct is that it combines the strategic thinking of planning with the flexibility of reactivity. The agent isn’t mindlessly responding to stimuli, but it also isn’t locked into assumptions that might prove wrong. Each cycle of reasoning incorporates new information from the previous action, allowing the agent to course-correct continuously.”

“computation should be a much more visible process when agents are involved. In other AI patterns, computation is visualized primarily as latency. But in agentic processes, as you can see in Figure 6-8, the computation step transforms from a single opaque moment into a sequence of visible operations.”

“Decomposition is most useful when a task has genuine complexity. Revealing how the agent breaks a goal into parts helps users see both the scope of work and how their intent has been interpreted, turning uncertainty into expectation.”

“Once a plan is in place and the agent begins to act, the designer’s focus shifts to communicating progress. At this stage, the interface is no longer helping users decide what to do but is helping them stay oriented as work unfolds over time.”

“Layered progress communication allows designers to support this range without overwhelming the interface or assuming constant attention.”

“When systems can act on their own, the interface becomes less about facilitating individual exchanges and more about maintaining a productive partnership over time.”

“The problem isn’t that agents misunderstand requests (though that happens) but that users can’t tell whether the agent has understood correctly until work is already underway or complete”

“trust gap right at the moment when trust matters most: when the user is handing over control.”

“The key is ensuring that users can always trace how their intent translated into action when that traceability matters.”

“The solution is aggressive prioritization based on what users actually need to know at each moment.”

“The goal is making autonomy feel like assistance rather than replacement. When designed well, shared control creates a productive partnership where agents handle routine work while humans provide judgment, creativity, and strategic direction. Users feel that they’re working with a capable assistant who respects their authority rather than an autonomous system that might act against their interests.”

“The agentic patterns we examined create new design challenges that didn’t exist with simpler AI. Reflection adds latency that must be explained. Tool use requires permission models that balance access with security. Planning needs review mechanisms that don’t slow everything down. Multiagent coordination must be comprehensible without being overwhelming. And adaptive problem solving requires interfaces that support iteration and redirection.”
